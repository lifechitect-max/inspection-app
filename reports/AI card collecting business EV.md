# Buying edges, not AI, make cards pay

Yes, a solo operator can run a positive-expected-value card business in 2026, but only a narrow set of models qualify, and AI is not what makes them work. The models that clear fees, taxes and the cycle all rest on **buying below market**. That means collections, shows, estates, mislisted lots and cross-language sourcing, followed by selective grading and selling through low-fee channels. The models built on holding modern product, paying full-price grading, flipping in-print sealed at retail, or running randomized breaks are negative-EV or carry tail risk that wipes out their margin. The backdrop is a split, late-cycle market. Transaction volume is at all-time highs (Whatnot did more than $8B of GMV in the first half of 2026 alone, eBay's focus categories grew 26% led by trading cards, and 26.6M cards were graded in 2025). At the same time, prices for mass-printed modern product are already falling 10–50%. Volume-based businesses are therefore doing better than appreciation-based ones, and they would be exposed if volume rolls over. AI has commoditized the visible layer of the hobby: card scanning, price lookup, listing text and underpriced-listing alerts are free or nearly free, and eBay now bans scraping and bot buying. Its real value to a solo operator is as a private decision engine for which cards to buy, which to grade and where, and how to read Japanese listings. It is not a product to sell. Realistic earnings are modest. On the cited fee math, a part-time flipper with $3,000 of capital earns roughly **$7–14 per hour before tax**, and every published success story carries heavy survivor bias.

## Record volume hides falling prices for modern product

Every measurable platform set records in 2025–26. Whatnot's live-sales GMV exceeded **$8B in 2025, then exceeded $8B again in H1 2026**, and in August 2026 it raised $545M at a **$20B valuation** ([Inc.](https://www.inc.com/jennifer-conrad/whatnot-just-clinched-a-20-billion-valuation/91386777); [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/small-business/articles/whatnot-raises-545mn-series-g-150151629.html)). eBay's Q2 2026 GMV rose 14% to $22.4B. Its focus categories grew **26%**, with collectibles the largest contributor, "driven primarily by trading cards" ([eBay Q2'26 presentation](https://ebay.q4cdn.com/610426115/files/doc_financials/2026/q2/2026_Q2_eBay_Earnings_Presentation_Final.pdf)). GameStop's collectibles sales rose **57% to $356.3M** and are now 45% of the company's revenue ([GameStop 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001326380/000132638026000050/a991-fy26q2earningsrelease.htm)). Grading hit **26.6M cards in 2025**, the highest since tracking began, with PSA at 19.26M (+26%) ([SI](https://www.si.com/collectibles/over-26-million-cards-graded-in-2025-how-the-market-exploded)). Adults drive the buying: **19% of US adults bought Pokémon cards for themselves** in a six-month window, and only about a quarter of them play the game ([Retail Brew/Circana](https://www.retailbrew.com/stories/2025/06/06/collectible-trading-cards-and-adults-drive-toy-sales-in-2025-circana)).

Prices tell a different story. Card Ladder's sports indexes were down over twelve months as of mid-2026: basketball −2%, football −6%, baseball about −8%, hockey −11%. The source describes this as "a general correction rather than a collapse," with vintage baseball still strong ([Athlon mid-year report](https://athlonsports.com/collectibles/2026-mid-year-collectibles-report-sports-cards-tcg)). Modern Pokémon is correcting despite retail sellouts. Chase cards and sealed product are about **20–50% off** their late-2025 speculative peaks ([PokemonPriceTracker](https://www.pokemonpricetracker.com/blog/posts/pokemon-card-market-crash-2026-what-really-happened)). Sealed product has averaged a **15–25% decline since March 2025**, and in-print sets such as Destined Rivals and Surging Sparks trade "at or slightly below MSRP" ([Misprint](https://www.misprint.com/posts/pokemon-sealed-product-price-trends-2026)). The cause is supply. The Pokémon Company printed about **10B cards** in the year to March 2026, which it describes as maximum capacity, against roughly 1.5–2B a year before 2019 ([My Nintendo News](https://mynintendonews.com/2026/05/31/the-pokemon-company-says-10-billion-pokemon-cards-were-printed-in-the-last-year/)). In sports, Fanatics/Topps now holds MLB, NBA and NFL licenses (NFL since April 1, 2026), so a single licensor controls modern sports supply ([Yahoo/Athlon](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/inside-fanatics-panini-feud-license-222501114.html)). The top end, meanwhile, keeps setting records. A PSA 10 Pikachu Illustrator sold for **$16.49M** in February 2026 ([ICv2](https://icv2.com/articles/news/view/61641/logan-pauls-pokemon-tcg-pikachu-illustrator-card-becomes-most-expensive-trading-card-ever-sold)).

The signals conflict. One Card Ladder Pokémon index was reportedly up 116% over a similar window, likely because it weights high-end and vintage cards, and some September 2026 "prices dropping" posts come from low-quality SEO sites. Still, the pattern is consistent. Record volume combined with falling prices for mass-printed product looks like a late boom in which supply is catching up. A 2022-style break would require volume to roll over as well, and nothing visible through Q2 2026 shows that yet. History shows how large the downside is. In the 2022 correction, modern icons fell **72–73% from February 2021** (Kobe 1996 Topps PSA 10, Jordan 1986 Fleer PSA 9) while vintage held ([SI](https://www.si.com/collectibles/news/5-superstar-rookie-cards-that-have-lost-the-most-value-since-2020); [Sports Collectors Digest](https://sportscollectorsdigest.com/news/sports-card-market-values-vintage-cards-modern-pwcc-card-ladder)). For a solo operator this splits the business models in two. Models that earn a spread on each transaction benefit from today's volume no matter which way prices move. Models that need modern prices to rise are fighting the printing presses.

## Fees take 6–16%, so the buy price decides profit

Fee schedules from secondary 2026 sources (primary pages could not be loaded) show that the selling channel alone moves margin by about nine points. eBay charges roughly **13.25%** on trading cards, calculated on item plus shipping plus tax, plus $0.30–0.40 per order ([Synder](https://synder.com/fees-ebay/final-value-fee/)). eBay's own help page lists 12.7% for most collectibles, so check the category rate ([eBay](https://www.ebay.com/help/selling/fees-credits-invoices/selling-fees?id=4822)). Whatnot charges an **8% commission plus about 2.9% + $0.30 processing**. A new volume-tiered schedule that took effect September 21, 2026 lowers the commission to about 6–6.5% for the largest public tiers, and sources disagree on the floor ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/09/17/whatnot-new-commission-rates-lower-three-percent/); [Voolist](https://www.voolist.com/blog/whatnot-fees-2026)). TCGplayer raised its commission to **10.75%** in February 2026, plus 2.5% + $0.30 ([TCGplayer help](https://help.tcgplayer.com/hc/en-us/articles/201357836-Fees)). Fanatics Collect charges **6%** on Buy Now listings priced below 120% of market, or 0% with FanCash payouts ([Fanatics Collect](https://support.fanaticscollect.com/en_us/buy-now-fees-ry33QCXaxe.md); [SI](https://www.si.com/collectibles/fanatics-collect-eliminates-seller-fees-new-fancash-payouts-program)). Courtyard charges no seller fee ([thecardshopfinder](https://thecardshopfinder.com/guides/pack-ripping/courtyard-io-review/)).

| Channel | Approx. fee on a $100 raw single | Net on a flip bought at $70 (after ~$5 postage, supplies) |
|---|---|---|
| eBay (trading cards) | ~$15.43 (15.4%) | ~$14 (≈20% return on cost) |
| TCGplayer Level 1–4 | ~$13.70 | ~$16 |
| Whatnot standard | ~$11.58 | ~$18 |
| Mercari | ~$10.50 | ~$19 |
| Fanatics Collect Buy Now | ~$6 (or $0 in FanCash) | ~$23.50 |

*Fee and net figures are calculated from the cited fee schedules, assuming $5 shipping charged to the buyer, about $8 sales tax on the eBay order, about $5 actual postage and $0.50 supplies.*

These numbers show where profit comes from. A card bought at 70% of market and sold on eBay nets about **14% of the sale price**. Buying the same card at 50–60% of market, which is common when buying whole collections and estates, roughly doubles the margin, a far bigger effect than any choice of channel. Low-value singles are a trap: a $2 TCGplayer sale loses about **28%** to fees before postage, and TCGplayer Direct now takes **50%** of the value of cards under $2.49 ([TCGplayer seller blog](https://seller.tcgplayer.com/blog/simpler-and-more-predictable-fees-coming-for-tcgplayer-direct-june-18)). Scaling the flip math to a part-time operator gives a sobering figure. Assume 10 hours a week, $3,000 of capital turning about 8.7 times a year (about $26k of sales) and a 14% net margin. That works out to roughly **$3,600 a year, or about $7/hour**, rising to about $14/hour with better sourcing. A forum estimate puts all-in taxes at about 50% of a reseller's net profit ([Elite Fourum](https://elitefourum.com/t/help-with-tax-questions-in-regards-to-pokemon-based-in-us/30564)). The federal 1099-K threshold went back to more than $20,000 and 200 transactions, but that changes paperwork, not liability ([CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2026/02/18/2026-tax-filing-and-form-1099-k-know-the-new-rules-this-tax-season/178331/)).

Japanese sourcing still offers a structural spread, but tariffs have narrowed it. Since August 2025, Japanese imports carry a **15% tariff** and the $800 de minimis exemption is gone. In one worked example, a Japanese booster box lands at **$106.95** ($78 + $15 shipping + $13.95 tariff), against $130–150 for an English box. A $20 single, however, can nearly double in cost once a carrier's $15 processing fee is added ([CardCodex](https://cardcodex.com/blog/us-tariffs-influence-pokemon-card-hobby/)). The two boxes are different products, so after resale fees the realistic net is closer to **5–20% per box**. The gap that survives is in tiers global buyers ignore: one SEO source reports Illustration Rares at a **$4.62 Japanese median vs. $12.07 Western**, while chase tiers have already been arbitraged flat ([thecardshopfinder](https://thecardshopfinder.com/guides/pokemon-card-investing/japanese-vs-english-pokemon/)). That figure comes from a single unverified source.

## Grading arbitrage flips negative at $80 per card

Grading looks like the obvious AI play, because computer vision can screen centering and surfaces before you pay a grader. In 2026, though, grading capacity matters more than screening skill. PSA raised prices in February 2026 (Value Bulk to $24.99, Value to $32.99, Regular to $79.99) ([DK Network](https://dknetwork.draftkings.com/2026/02/10/psa-grading-price-increase-2026/)). On **June 2, 2026 it paused all four Value tiers** after its backlog surged, with the peak reported at 12–14M cards. The tiers reopen when the backlog falls to about 5M ([PreGradeCards](https://pregradecards.com/blog/psa-pauses-value-tiers-june-2026); [JustCollect](https://blog.justcollect.com/psa-pauses-card-grading-on-value-tiers-due-to-demand)). PSA's tracker read **9.9M on September 8**, and PSA said it might reopen one Value tier before the end of September. Whether it did could not be confirmed ([PSA Backlog Tracker](https://www.psacard.com/info/backlog-tracker); [SI](https://www.si.com/collectibles/psa-backlog-falls-12-million-means-card-grading-2026)). Alternatives are limited. Beckett's cheap tiers were paused, TAG's cheapest open tier was about $150, and CGC Bulk costs **$17 but takes 150 working days** ([pregradecards](https://pregradecards.com/blog/psa-beckett-both-paused-where-to-grade-cards-august-2026); [cardgrade.io](https://cardgrade.io/cgc-card-grading-cost)).

Gem rates set the ceiling. In H1 2025, TCG submissions came back PSA 10 **50%** of the time and sports cards **34%** ([CLLCT](https://www.cllct.com/sports-collectibles/sports-cards/how-many-cards-grade-as-10s-gem-rate-at-43-so-far-in-2025)). These are cards that submitters already chose to send, so a buyer of random raw cards will do worse. Premiums have also narrowed: modern PSA 10s now sell for only **1.3–2.5x a PSA 9**, because populations have reached the tens of thousands ([pokemonpricetracker](https://www.pokemonpricetracker.com/blog/posts/psa-10-vs-psa-9-is-the-price-gap-worth-it-in-2026)). Putting the cited fees and gem rates into a simple expected-value model shows how the result flips sign:

| Scenario (assumed values) | Grading cost | Expected profit per card |
|---|---|---|
| Modern Pokémon SIR, raw $40, PSA 10 $150, 50% gem, sold on eBay | PSA Value $32.99 | **+$2 (3% ROI)** over 2–3 months |
| Same card, sold on Fanatics Collect (6%) | PSA Value $32.99 | **+$12 (15%)** |
| Same card | PSA Regular $79.99 | **−$45** |
| Sports card, raw $60, PSA 10 $300, 34% gem, eBay | PSA Value | **+$31 (32%)** |
| Same sports card | PSA Regular $79.99 | **−$16** |

The rule of thumb that follows: at a $20–35 all-in grading cost, a card needs a PSA 10 worth roughly **3–4x raw** and a realistic gem chance of at least 40%. At $80, the PSA 10 must be worth at least about $250 more than the raw card. Grading arbitrage is a **positive-EV business only when cheap tiers are open, when screening is tight enough to beat the self-selected gem rates, and when the operator sells through low-fee channels**. AI pre-grading apps cost **$0.25–0.33 per card** and claim about 89% agreement with PSA within ±1 grade, but those benchmarks are self-published by vendors ([PreGradeCards](https://pregradecards.com/blog/best-ai-card-grading-apps-compared-2026)). They help as a filter, though a ±1-grade error is exactly the gap between profit and loss on a 10-or-bust card. Building a grading company is closed. Collectors (PSA, SGC and Beckett since December 2025) controls about **79% of grading volume** and owns Genamint's AI fingerprinting technology ([Collectors](https://www.collectors.com/company-announcement); [Action Network](https://www.actionnetwork.com/news/psa-purchases-genamint-improve-card-grading-april-21)).

## Breaks, sealed, vending and bulk are thin or fragile

Live breaking has real gross margin and a growing legal tail. Breakers typically sell spots for **20–50% more than product cost**, and hype breaks go far higher ([cardpulse](https://www.cardpulse.club/blog/card-breaks-explained-2026); [thecardshopfinder](https://thecardshopfinder.com/guides/sports-cards/card-breaks-guide/)). Take a $1,000 pick-your-team break sold at 1.4x on Whatnot. It nets about **$205 (14.6% of revenue)** after commission, processing and supplies. At 1.2x it nets about **$29**, and any spot the breaker has to cover turns it negative. The model only works at volume, with product bought at distributor cost. Volume tiers help large breakers most, so the gap between them and small breakers grows. There are also allegations that manufacturers give favored large breakers better hit distribution ([Cardlines](https://cardlines.com/topps-chrome-sapphire-hit-parade-reignites-new-backyard-breaks-preferential-treatment-allegations/)). The legal exposure is specific. Attorney Paul Lesko filed arbitration demands alleging that randomized breaks are illegal lotteries under California Penal Code 319/319.3, and the claimant count grew from 15 to **nearly 70 by June**. A California False Claims Act suit naming Whatnot and Fanatics Live has also been unsealed ([Value Added Resource](https://www.valueaddedresource.net/whatnot-arbitration-claims-breaking-practices/); [Baseball America](https://www.baseballamerica.com/stories/lawsuit-accuses-breaking-platform-whatnot-of-being-unregulated-online-casino-for-sports-cards-report/)). eBay has already limited repacks to vetted live sellers ([VAR](https://www.valueaddedresource.net/ebay-trading-card-repack-rules-live/)). Random-team formats earn the highest margin and are the most exposed. Fixed-price pick-your-team breaks look safer. The outcome of these cases as of October 2026 is unknown.

Buying sealed product to hold, or flipping in-print sealed product bought at retail, is negative-EV in this cycle. In-print sets already trade at or below MSRP, and individual products such as Phantasmal Flames ETBs fell **25% in ten days** ([Misprint](https://www.misprint.com/posts/pokemon-sealed-product-price-trends-2026)). The exception is a set that goes out of print while demand holds: Evolving Skies boxes rose from about $220 to $260–310 in a year. That pattern is hard to predict and slow to pay out. Retail scarcity at MSRP is real: the Pokémon 30th Celebration wave sold out within minutes on September 16, 2026, with limits of 2 per SKU ([Swisher Post](https://www.swisherpost.com/gaming/pokemon-30th-anniversary-target-restock-limits/)). Purchase limits cap that opportunity at hobby scale.

The other "picks and shovels" models are smaller than their marketing suggests. Card shops now earn about **20% on cards** but **65–70% on accessories** ([VMFS guide](https://vmfsusa.com/blogs/business/pokemon-card-vending-machine-the-complete-2026-operator-guide)), and closures are common, citing online competition and falling sales ([Sports Collectors Daily](https://www.sportscollectorsdaily.com/tag/sports-card-shop-closing/)). Vending machine claims of **$600–2,500 per month and 35–55% net margins** come entirely from vendors selling machines ([VMFS USA](https://vmfsusa.com/blogs/business/is-the-pokemon-vending-machine-business-profitable)). Pokémon runs about 1,473 of its own machines and will not sell them. After theft, downtime and sourcing problems, an honest estimate is $300–900 a month per machine, and no independent profit-and-loss data exists. Bulk trading pays **$8–10 per 1,000 cards** to buy and $25–60 to sell sorted lots ([Knight and Day Games](https://knightanddaygames.com/pages/sell-us-your-bulk); [pokemonpricetracker](https://www.pokemonpricetracker.com/pokemon-cards-cost)), which leaves minimum wage or less unless the real business is picking valuable cards out of the bulk. Show tables cost **$75–500** at regional and mid-size shows, and total show costs run 2–4x the table fee ([thecardshopfinder](https://thecardshopfinder.com/guides/card-shows/sell-at-card-shows/)). A $150 table moving $3,000 of inventory at a 20–30% gross margin nets about $150–450 for the day. The real value of a table is the walk-up sellers it brings in.

## AI is commoditized at the surface and valuable in decisions

Most of the obvious AI products already exist, are free, or are against platform terms. eBay's AI card scanning has passed **80M+ cumulative scans** ([eBay Q2'26](https://ebay.q4cdn.com/610426115/files/doc_financials/2026/q2/2026_Q2_eBay_Earnings_Presentation_Final.pdf)), and its "magical listing" tool writes titles, item specifics and prices from photos ([eBay Innovation](https://innovation.ebayinc.com/tech/features/magical-listing-tool-harnesses-the-power-of-ai-to-make-selling-on-ebay-faster-easier-and-more-accurate/)). CollX has millions of users and about **$10M in revenue** at $10–25 a month ([Technical.ly](https://technical.ly/entrepreneurship/collx-trading-card-marketplace-profitable/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/07/collx-raises-10m-to-grow-its-card-collection-marketplace)). Collectr bootstrapped to an "eight-figure business" ([BetaKit](https://betakit.com/how-collectr-bootstrapped-a-trading-card-hobby-into-an-eight-figure-business/)), and the App Store is full of clone scanners. Deal alerts are free: CardSniper and Figoca browser extensions, a no-paywall PokeDealFinder Discord bot tracking 40k+ cards, and Apify scrapers ([CardSniper](https://cardsniper.gumroad.com/l/gsqdd); [top.gg](https://top.gg/bot/1501241952908218511)). Any edge from being first to a mispriced listing is therefore shared with thousands of other users. eBay has also closed the automation path. Its June 2025 API license bars feeding restricted pricing and sales data into generative-AI tools, and its user agreement, effective **February 20, 2026**, prohibits "automated scraping, buy-for-me agents, LLM-driven bots, or any end-to-end flow that attempts to place orders without human review" ([EcommerceBytes](https://www.ecommercebytes.com/2025/07/18/ebay-restricts-developers-from-using-its-data-to-train-ai/); [Shopifreaks](https://www.shopifreaks.com/ebay-bans-unapproved-agentic-shopping-bots-from-its-platform-set-stage-for-more-partnerships/)). Building another scanner, price index, alert bot or pre-grader to sell is negative-EV for a solo founder. Even the CollX benchmark implies only about 2% of users pay.

Real gaps remain where the incumbents' tools fail. Scanners get the card name right but the **variant wrong** (finish, parallel, edition), and "the variant requirement is where most scanners lose points" ([EyevoTCG](https://eyevotcg.com/blog/best-pokemon-card-scanner-apps-2026/)). eBay's bulk AI listing tool has at times failed to identify brand, card number and player ([Value Added Resource](https://www.valueaddedresource.net/ebay-bulk-ai-listing-sports-cards-broken/)). Condition-adjusted pricing for raw cards and Japanese-language market knowledge still live mostly in forum threads ([Elite Fourum](https://www.elitefourum.com/t/the-giant-japanese-market-thread/31400?page=312)). That points to where a solo operator should use AI. The most promising uses are a private grading-triage pipeline (scan, condition model, live raw/PSA/CGC spreads, then choice of grader and tier) and LLM translation plus image identification to search Mercari JP and Yahoo Auctions JP. Vision models can also catch mislisted lots in long-tail categories that keyword bots miss, and a structured listing pipeline can extract parallel, serial, set code and language more accurately than eBay's native tool. Each of these lowers buying cost or raises sell-through for the operator's own inventory, or underpins a done-for-you service such as consignment or submission management. None of them is a moat, and all of them depend on a human placing every order.

Fraud raises the value of trust, and AI cuts both ways. PSA reports intercepting **more than $200M** in counterfeit and altered items, with counterfeit Pokémon cards up **125%** and altered Pokémon cards up **407%** year over year ([SI](https://www.si.com/collectibles/psa-report-reveals-200m-fake-cards)). Scammers now clone real cert numbers onto fake slabs ([Slabfy](https://slabfy.com/blog/how-to-spot-fake-graded-slabs)). Since October 2025 there have been more than 600 reported Pokémon e-commerce scams costing over $1.1M, many run through polished fake Shopify stores and AI-generated images ([McAfee](https://www.mcafee.com/blogs/security-news/this-week-in-scams-pokemon-card-scams-email-extortion-ai-wedding-photo/)). For a buyer, AI-assisted image forensics and cert checks are basic defensive hygiene. For a seller, a verifiable identity, consistent photography and checked provenance are a modest but durable selling point, worth more as cheap AI fakes spread.

## Scorecard of solo models by expected value

| Business model | EV verdict (fall 2026) | What decides it |
|---|---|---|
| Sourcing-led flipping (collections, estates, shows at 50–70% of market) | **+EV, modest** (~$7–14/hr part-time) | Buy price and turnover; low-fee channels add ~9 points |
| Vintage / blue-chip dealing | **+EV with expertise** | Held value in 2022; requires authentication skill and capital |
| Japan-sourced TCG (AI translation + ID) | **Thin +EV** (~5–20%/box) | 15% tariff, carrier fees; target tiers not already arbitraged |
| Raw-to-graded with AI triage at cheap tiers | **Small +EV** (3–32% per card) | Needs Value-tier access, gem rate ≥40%, PSA 10 ≥3–4x raw |
| Grading at $80+ tiers | **−EV** for most modern cards | PSA 10 premium must exceed ~$250 |
| Holding modern sealed / modern singles | **−EV** | 10B/yr print runs; 15–50% drawdowns underway |
| Retail-to-secondary in-print sealed | **≈0 to −EV** | Trades at/below MSRP; purchase limits cap volume |
| Pick-your-team breaks at volume | **Thin +EV** (~15% of revenue) | Distributor allocation, spot fill rate, platform tier |
| Randomized breaks / repacks | **+gross margin, −risk-adjusted** | California lottery claims, qui tam, eBay repack limits |
| Bulk sorting without picking | **≈0** | ~$0–30 per 1,000 cards before labor |
| Vending machines | **Unproven** | Only vendor-supplied profit claims exist |
| Selling a generic AI scanner, alert bot or pre-grader | **−EV** | Free incumbents, eBay ToS, ~2% paid conversion |
| AI-enabled services (submission triage, consignment listing, show-floor pricing) | **Plausibly +EV, unvalidated** | No willingness-to-pay data; fits volume-led market |

The weakest link in all of these estimates is the evidence on operator outcomes. Whatnot's claim that "66% of sellers earn more than $10k/month" comes from a self-selected survey of active sellers ([ecommercebonsai](https://ecommercebonsai.com/whatnot-statistics/)). Flipping success stories are small and promotional, and no dataset on how many resellers quit or lose money was found. The scorecard values come from cited fee schedules and gem rates combined with assumed card prices. They show the shape of the economics and are not forecasts.

## Conclusion

The question "can AI make cards a +EV business?" is better asked as "can AI lower my cost of buying and deciding?" The toll collectors already have the structural margin. Fanatics licenses, prints and runs a marketplace. Collectors grades about four of every five cards. eBay and Whatnot take 6–16% of every sale, and all three are building AI into their platforms for free while restricting outside bots. What a solo operator can own is judgment applied at scale: screening hundreds of raw cards to find the few worth $33 of grading, reading Japanese listings that English-speaking competitors skip, and catching a mislabeled parallel in a lot. Those uses of AI compound a sourcing edge. They do not create one.

The timing argument points the same way. The money is in transaction volume, not in rising modern prices. So the most robust design is a low-inventory, fast-turnover operation that earns a spread or a fee on each card and holds little modern stock. That design survives a price correction, and its main exposure is the volume rollover that has not arrived yet. Watch Whatnot's growth rate, PSA's monthly submissions and eBay's focus-category growth as early warnings. Assume that any edge which can be automated, such as alerts, scanning or listing text, will be competed away within months.
